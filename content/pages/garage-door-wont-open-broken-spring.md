Your garage door just stopped working. Before you call anyone, it helps to know which of two very different problems you have: a **broken spring**, which is a safety issue and a job for a professional, or an **opener, remote, power, or sensor** problem, which is often a simple fix. This page walks through how to tell them apart, what is safe to check, and what repairs typically cost.

*General information only, not professional repair advice. Prices come from national cost guides and vary by location. Call to confirm a quote before work starts.*

## First, what the spring does

A garage door is heavy. The springs carry most of that weight, so the opener only has to guide the door rather than lift it. Door maker Clopay puts it this way: "The spring literally does the heavy lifting and lowering of the garage door, and it only lasts so long" ([Clopay](https://www.clopaydoor.com/blog/post/blog/2021/03/05/do-i-have-a-broken-garage-door-spring)). Clopay also says most springs are designed to last 10,000 cycles, with one cycle being one trip up and one trip down, and that springs tend to break most often in cold weather and while the door is down.

## Signs it is a broken spring

Clopay describes the classic signs ([Clopay](https://www.clopaydoor.com/blog/post/blog/2021/03/05/do-i-have-a-broken-garage-door-spring)):

- **A very loud bang.** If you were home, it sounded like a "POP-bang-gunfire" from the garage, per Clopay.
- **A gap in a torsion spring.** Standard torsion springs sit centered above the door opening. Look up from the floor: if the coil is split into two pieces with a distinct gap, it has broken. Don't touch it.
- **A stretched or uneven extension spring.** Extension springs run along the top of the side tracks. Clopay says they will look stretched when the door is closed, or have uneven spacing.
- **Two springs, one broken.** If your door has two springs, only one may have snapped. Clopay says both should be replaced at the same time.

Opener makers list the same cause for several symptoms. LiftMaster's troubleshooting section says the springs may be broken when the opener motor "hums briefly, then won't work," when the opener doesn't operate from either the wall control or the remote (after you've checked power and locks), and when the opener strains to operate the door ([LiftMaster 8500W guide](https://www.bartondoor.com/uploads/product-manuals/documents/Liftmaster-8500-20200603.pdf)).

## Signs it is the opener, remote, power, or sensors instead

These are the checks from the LiftMaster troubleshooting section. Each is safe to do from the floor with the door not moving ([LiftMaster 8500W guide](https://www.bartondoor.com/uploads/product-manuals/documents/Liftmaster-8500-20200603.pdf)):

| What you see | What to check |
|---|---|
| Nothing works from the wall control or the remote | Does the opener have power? Plug a lamp into the same outlet. If it doesn't light, check the fuse box or breaker. Some outlets are controlled by a wall switch. |
| Opener hums or runs but the door doesn't move | Make sure any door lock is disabled. If it still won't lift, the spring may be broken (see above). |
| Wall control works but the remote doesn't | Check that the lock feature on the wall control is not turned on, then reprogram the remote. Check your own model's manual for the steps. |
| Door won't close and the opener lights blink | The manual says the safety reversing sensor must be connected and aligned correctly before the opener will move the door down. Verify the sensors are properly installed, aligned, and free of obstructions. |
| Door won't move when the weather is freezing | LiftMaster notes ice or snow under the door can freeze it to the floor. Clear any build-up. |

Sensor problems are cheap relative to springs. HomeAdvisor lists sensor realignment at $150 to $250 and reprogramming a remote at $100 to $350 ([HomeAdvisor](https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/garages/repair-a-garage-door/)).

## Quick safe checks (and when to skip them)

1. **Stop pressing the button.** If the motor hums but the door doesn't move, strain on the opener is not helping. Unplug it if you can do so safely.
2. **Look, don't touch.** From the floor, look at the spring above the door and the cables on each side. A visible gap or loose cables means stop here and call a pro.
3. **Keep people and cars out from under the door,** especially if it's partly open. The federal safety wording for openers says: "NEVER GO UNDER A STOPPED PARTIALLY OPEN DOOR" ([16 CFR 1211.16](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title16-vol2/pdf/CFR-2024-title16-vol2-sec1211-16.pdf)).
4. **Only if you saw no sign of a broken spring and the door is closed:** you can do the balance check that manufacturers and the CPSC describe. Pull the emergency release to disconnect the door from the opener, then lift it by hand. CPSC says a balanced door "will stay in place when stopped in any partially-open position," and that doors that aren't balanced, or that bind or stick, should be serviced by a professional ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/garage.pdf)). Genie's version has you lift the door about 36 inches; if it moves more than a few inches either way, it is not properly balanced ([Genie](https://www.geniecompany.com/garage-door-opener-safety)). If it's heavy, or won't stay put, treat it as a spring or balance problem and call a pro.

## Why not to DIY a torsion spring

This is the one place every source agrees.

- Genie: "Do not remove, repair, or adjust springs or anything to which door-spring parts are fastened, such as wood blocks, steel brackets, cables, or any like items." These repairs must be made by a professional dealer with proper tools and instructions ([Genie](https://www.geniecompany.com/garage-door-opener-safety)).
- LiftMaster's manual says all repairs to cables, spring assemblies and other hardware, "ALL of which are under EXTREME tension," must be made by a trained door systems technician ([LiftMaster 8500W guide](https://www.bartondoor.com/uploads/product-manuals/documents/Liftmaster-8500-20200603.pdf)).
- Clopay: "Standard torsion spring repair is not a DIY project," and the task should be left to people with the proper equipment and expertise ([Clopay](https://www.clopaydoor.com/blog/post/blog/2021/03/05/do-i-have-a-broken-garage-door-spring)).
- HomeAdvisor: "Because springs hold hundreds of pounds of tension, leave this job to a pro" ([HomeAdvisor](https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/garages/repair-a-garage-door/)).

The same warning covers cables, brackets, and drums attached to the spring system, not just the spring itself.

## Getting the car out

This is the hard part, and the honest answer depends on whether the spring is broken.

- **If you suspect a broken spring (bang, gap in the coil, very heavy door):** Genie says: "Do not operate your garage door, either manually or with the opener, if the door has a broken spring" ([Genie](https://www.geniecompany.com/garage-door-opener-safety)). The reason is in the opener manuals: LiftMaster warns that weak or broken springs "could result in an open door falling rapidly and/or unexpectedly" when you pull the emergency release ([LiftMaster 8500W guide](https://www.bartondoor.com/uploads/product-manuals/documents/Liftmaster-8500-20200603.pdf)). If your car is stuck inside, call a repair company, say it is stuck, and ask for the earliest slot.
- **If the spring looks fine and it is a power outage or opener problem:** LiftMaster says to use the emergency release handle only when the door is closed, if possible, and only when the doorway is clear of people and objects. Pull down on the handle until you hear a click, then operate the door by hand. LiftMaster also warns never to use the release handle to pull the door open or closed, because if the rope knot comes untied you could fall. When power is back, pull the handle straight down a second time to reconnect; the door reconnects on the next up or down operation ([LiftMaster 8500W guide](https://www.bartondoor.com/uploads/product-manuals/documents/Liftmaster-8500-20200603.pdf)).

## What repairs typically cost

HomeAdvisor reports an average garage door repair cost of $265, with most prices between $155 and $379, and a possible range from $80 up to about $700 ([HomeAdvisor](https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/garages/repair-a-garage-door/)). By part:

| Part | HomeAdvisor cost range |
|---|---|
| Springs | $180 to $350 |
| Opener (repair) | $100 to $500 |
| Cables | $100 to $400 |
| Track | $5 to $400 |

HomeAdvisor says labor runs $75 to $150 per hour and to budget for emergency service fees if the door fails outside regular business hours. It doesn't give a figure for that fee, so ask when you call. Its data comes from a survey of over 30,000 customers; actual quotes depend on your door, your area, and the problem.

These are national averages, not quotes. Call at least a few local companies and ask each for a price in writing before work begins.

## How to vet a repair company (and avoid bait-and-switch)

The BBB says garage door repair scams are common and that many target homeowners with urgent repairs. It reports that scam companies often advertise a low fee for the service call, then demand much more for additional "repairs," and can appear at the top of internet searches for emergency garage door repair ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/article/scams/29292-bbb-scam-alert-garage-door-repair-scams)). Its warning signs:

1. **Generic or multiple business names** that are not registered with the state.
2. **No physical address**, only a phone number or email.
3. **No ID, uniform, or marked vehicle.**
4. **Unrealistically low prices** for the service call.
5. **High-pressure tactics** that insist you can't wait.
6. **Payment in full upfront.** BBB says reputable companies provide a detailed estimate and let you pay after the work is finished.

BBB's tips for protecting yourself:

- **Check the company.** When they answer, ask for their address and compare it to the ad. Search the name and address with "complaint," "review," or "scam," and check BBB.org.
- **Get multiple estimates** so you can spot an outlier.
- **Ask for proof of insurance, licensing, qualifications, and ID.** Licensing requirements vary by location, so check with your local authorities.
- **Get everything in writing**: scope of work, materials, and costs, before any work begins.
- **Don't be rushed.** "Scammers thrive on urgency."

A practical use of the cost table above: if a quote for a spring replacement is several times the HomeAdvisor range, ask for a line-by-line written breakdown and get a second quote before agreeing.

## Quick recap

1. Loud bang, gap in the coil, or a very heavy door: it's the spring. Don't touch it, and don't operate the door.
2. Dead opener with no bang: check power, locks, remote batteries, and sensors first.
3. Never DIY torsion springs, cables, or drums.
4. Get a written quote, verify the company, and don't pay everything upfront.
5. Test your door every month: CPSC recommends inspecting the door and opener every 30 days, and testing that the door reverses when it hits a 2x4 laid flat on the floor ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/garage.pdf)).
